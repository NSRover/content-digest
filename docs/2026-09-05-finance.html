Gold Is Money Again — Even the Financial Establishment Now Admits It.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

A major shift in the mainstream financial narrative is underway as a UBS chief strategist declares that gold is money again, defying traditional valuation models and showing that its bull market has further to run. This resurgence is driven by the 2022 freezing of Russia's foreign reserves, which prompted central banks to seek sanctions-proof assets, alongside growing concerns over U.S. fiscal dominance and rising national debt. Consequently, gold is increasingly outperforming and replacing government bonds as the ultimate safe-haven asset.

Key points
  • In 2004, the Financial Times published an editorial claiming gold was a pointless "barbaric relic" destined to go the way of the dodo bird, but a recent article by UBS chief strategist Banu Baweji marks a complete reversal in the mainstream narrative by declaring gold is money again.
  • The historical relationship between gold and real yields broke down in February 2022 when Western governments froze $630 billion of Russia's foreign exchange reserves, prompting global reserve managers to realize that sovereign debt securities can become unavailable overnight.
  • Since the 2022 freeze, developing country central banks and sovereign wealth funds have increased their gold allocations from 5% of reserves to 11%, though this remains well below the 26% held by developed markets and the historical 40% norm for gold-backed monetary systems.
  • Gold is increasingly driven by structural factors, specifically the erosion of confidence in U.S. public finances and "fiscal dominance," where massive deficits and rising national debt pressure the Federal Reserve to keep interest rates lower than they otherwise would be.
  • The Dutch Central Bank is repatriating a significant portion of its gold reserves from New York and Ottawa back to Europe, moving some to London and some to a military base in Zeist, reflecting a desire to keep assets closer to home in an era of geopolitical uncertainty.
  • Norway's $2.2 trillion sovereign wealth fund is reportedly considering cutting its holdings of U.S. Treasury bonds, highlighting a broader global trend of institutional investors seeking alternatives to traditional government debt.
