The Return of Economic Populism and the End of Two Percent | Vincent Deluard
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

Vincent Deluard argues that the global economy is shifting into a new regime of economic populism, fiscal dominance, and national capitalism, which is permanently breaking the 30-year-old two percent inflation target. The massive shift in the political center of gravity—driven by voter fatigue over affordability, inequality, and foreign conflicts—is forcing governments toward structural deficit spending that central banks will ultimately have to underwrite. As a result, the era of massive foreign capital flows into US markets is beginning to unwind, posing severe structural risks to US asset valuations, the US dollar, and Treasury markets while creating a major headwind for the Mexican peso.

Key points
  • Economic populism has replaced traditional left-right cultural debates as the primary political vector, shifting focus toward class warfare, affordability, and the repatriation of national capital.
  • The traditional two percent inflation target is becoming obsolete due to structural fiscal deficits, digitization, subscription-based pricing models, and price discrimination that shift economic surplus from consumers to producers.
  • The "super peso" trade in Mexico is reaching its end as immigration slowdowns, tariff renegotiations, weaker tourism, and narrowing interest rate differentials reverse previous capital inflows.
  • Canada is emerging as an "anti-peso" beneficiary, backed by strong energy and fertilizer exports, pragmatic center-focused leadership, and a multi-trillion-dollar domestic pension pool that can be redirected from US markets into local infrastructure.
  • Global "national capitalism" poses a major threat to US equities and Treasuries, as countries like Canada, Japan, and European nations look to retain and repatriate domestic savings to finance their own domestic energy, grid, and defense requirements.
  • France is entering a peripheral-style debt and currency crisis at the core of the eurozone, driven by high debt-to-GDP, high foreign ownership of its bonds, and political paralysis between far-left and far-right movements.
