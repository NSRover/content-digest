A Once in a Lifetime Wealth Transfer Just Began.
gemini-flash (gemini-2.5-flash)fallback
TLDR

The US government, through the Treasury and Federal Reserve, appears to be pursuing a "hidden tax" strategy by increasing liquidity and inflation, despite public statements to the contrary. This strategy aims to reduce the real value of the national debt, but it disproportionately impacts the wealth of the poorest segments of the population while benefiting asset owners. Investors should prepare for a potential "liquidity shock" and increased inflationary pressures, even as net worth across the population may decline.

Key points
  • The US government's fiscal position is weak, with a growing debt and deficit, and public officials claim to be working to control spending and inflation. However, their actions, particularly regarding liquidity, suggest a different agenda.
  • The Treasury is increasing spending through a long-term bond buyback program, injecting "dry powder" from its General Account into the financial system, which adds liquidity. This contradicts public statements about cutting spending.
  • The Federal Reserve is expanding its balance sheet by buying short-term government bonds, creating new bank reserves and adding significant new liquidity to the financial system, despite claims of taming inflation.
  • The reverse repo market, which typically absorbs excess liquidity, is not effectively doing so because the interest rates offered are lower than short-term Treasury bills, meaning rate hikes are having little impact on overall liquidity.
  • The combined actions of the Treasury and Federal Reserve are projected to significantly increase America's net liquidity, leading to a "positive liquidity shock" similar to post-pandemic levels.
  • This increase in liquidity is closely linked to core inflation, suggesting that the government's actions will lead to more spending and higher inflation, which is the opposite of what officials publicly state they are trying to achieve.
  • This strategy, known as the Cantillon effect, acts as a "hidden tax" by reducing the purchasing power of money through inflation. While new liquidity initially benefits asset owners and large corporations, it eventually leads to higher consumer prices, eroding the real value of wages and savings for average citizens, effectively transferring wealth.

Going, Going Treasuries: The 40-Year Bond Era Is Over.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The 40-year bull market in government bonds has ended, signaling a transition into a bear market where Treasuries and other sovereign debt are losing their status as safe reserve assets. In this new paradigm of stagflation, weak economic data no longer guarantees falling yields, making long-term government bonds highly risky. Consequently, central banks and investors are increasingly turning to gold and silver as the ultimate monetary assets to protect against currency depreciation and debt devaluation.

Key points
  • The historic 40-year bull market in government bonds, which saw yields fall from their 1981 peak to the lows of the COVID-19 era, has officially ended, ushering in a challenging bond bear market.
  • The U.S. faces a massive refinancing burden, requiring approximately $9 trillion annually to roll over its national debt, a process that becomes a vicious circle as rising yields lower bond values and increase borrowing costs.
  • The traditional market dynamic where weak economic data automatically triggered lower yields and higher bond prices has broken down, indicating that the era of easy money is over and has been replaced by stagflation.
  • In a recent interview, President Trump acknowledged inflation as a mechanism that can pay down national debt quickly, raising concerns about the potential for inflationary devaluation of government debt.
  • Central banks, particularly in China, have been aggressively accumulating gold since 2022 as they recognize the declining value of Treasuries and seek to transition away from government bonds as primary reserve assets.
  • Investors are cautioned to avoid long-term government debt, with a recommendation to restrict any government bond exposure to maturities of less than one year to protect principal capital from rising interest rates.
