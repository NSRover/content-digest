China Is Dumping U.S. Treasuries for Gold — Here’s Why.

TLDR

China's holdings of U.S. Treasuries have fallen to their lowest level since 2008, a shift reminiscent of the 1971 run on the dollar that led to the closing of the gold window. In response to geopolitical tensions and the freezing of Russia's reserves, the People's Bank of China is actively diversifying away from U.S. debt and accelerating its accumulation of physical gold. This global shift away from the dollar as a reserve asset signals long-term inflationary pressures for the U.S., highlighting gold and silver as essential protective monetary assets.

Key points
  • China's U.S. Treasury holdings fell to $618 billion in July, down from a peak of over $1.3 trillion in November 2013, marking an 18-year low. This gradual divestment accelerated after the U.S. froze Russia's overseas reserves in 2022, prompting fears in Beijing and other nations of similar asset freezes.
  • The People's Bank of China has dramatically accelerated its official gold purchases throughout 2026, starting with 40,000 ounces in January and rising to 650,000 ounces (20 tons) in August. This consistent monthly increase positions China as a leader for the Global South in diversifying away from Western fiat assets.
  • The U.S. Treasury market faces additional pressure from Japan, where a weakening yen may force the Japanese to sell their own Treasury holdings to defend their currency. This dynamic is further complicated by political pressure from the U.S. Treasury on the Bank of Japan to raise interest rates.
  • Artificial demand for U.S. Treasuries is currently being maintained by highly leveraged UK-based hedge funds engaging in basis trades. This activity obscures the underlying vulnerability of the Treasury market, which is effectively skating on thin ice.
  • In the UK, the Bank of England opted not to raise interest rates alongside the Fed, ECB, and Bank of Japan, and instead slowed down its quantitative tightening operations due to trouble in the gilt market. This economic weakness and public sector expansion could lead to a weaker pound, which would drive the price of gold higher in sterling terms.
