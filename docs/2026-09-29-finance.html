Why the China Threat Is Overblown | Logan Wright

TLDR

China's economic problems are structural, not cyclical, stemming from the largest single-country credit expansion in history after the Global Financial Crisis. This credit boom, fueled by local governments and shadow banking with implicit government guarantees, has exhausted the financial system's capacity for growth, leading to persistent deflation and an impaired financial system. The prevailing narrative of China's inevitable rise and policymakers' ability to engineer a recovery is fundamentally mistaken, as official GDP data obscures the true scale of the slowdown and the collapse of the property sector.

Key points
  • China's economic instability began with a massive credit expansion between 2012 and 2016, following the Global Financial Crisis, where the money supply nearly doubled. This expansion was the largest in a single country in at least a century and was driven by indirect funding through the banking system.
  • Local governments played a crucial role in fueling this credit boom by borrowing extensively from banks to fund infrastructure and property projects, operating under the assumption of implicit government guarantees. This "moral hazard" was a deliberate strategy, encouraging continuous money creation.
  • The financial system transformed from a stable, deposit-funded system in 2012 to a more unstable one by 2016, with marginal funding coming from non-deposit instruments like wealth management products and marginal lending from shadow banks. This led to a "flight to risk" among investors, expecting government bailouts.
  • The expectation of government bailouts began to erode around 2018 with crackdowns on protests, signaling a shift from a perceived "weak" collective leadership that would compensate to a centralized power under Xi Jinping less inclined to intervene. This change led to a collapse in credit demand and a "flight to quality" in investments.
  • China's official GDP data hides the true scale of the economic slowdown, as it does not incorporate the collapse of the property sector, which represented 20-25% of GDP, and nothing has replaced it as a growth driver. The GDP target functions more as an internal signaling mechanism to maintain party coherence and project stability rather than an accurate reflection of economic reality.
  • The current economic strategy, characterized by an investment economy and weak domestic demand, leads China to prioritize expanding global export share and value-added. This approach views trade tensions as diplomatic problems rather than fundamental economic imbalances, further exacerbating structural issues.
