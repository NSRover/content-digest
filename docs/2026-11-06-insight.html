How War Shaped Human Evolution - David Geary

Guest

Dr. David Geary is an evolutionary psychologist, cognitive scientist, professor at the University of Missouri, and author. His expertise centers on cognitive development, the origin of the human mind, and how evolutionary pressures—particularly reproductive competition, tool use, and male coalitional conflict—have shaped human psychology and behavior. He is currently working on the second edition of his book, *The Origin of Mind*, for the American Psychological Association.

TLDR

Human intelligence, large brain size, and self-awareness evolved largely due to an evolutionary arms race driven by technological innovation, male coalitional violence, and intergroup conflict. Highly unequal reproductive success historically motivated men to seek dominance and organize into hierarchies, while women evolved social networks for mutual support and relational strategies. In the modern world, these ingrained drives for social status and control persist despite reduced physical threats, often causing perpetual anxiety and over-competitiveness unless consciously managed.

Key points
  • Human brain expansion began between 2 and 2.5 million years ago with *Homo habilis* and accelerated with *Homo erectus*, driven by technological advances like tool-making and cooking that increased calorie availability and population sizes, ultimately sparking intense intergroup competition.
  • Organized male coalitional violence has shaped human evolution for nearly two million years; this pattern peaked 5,000 to 7,000 years ago following the advent of agriculture, creating a genetic bottleneck where there were 17 female relatives for every male relative due to selective male-on-male extermination.
  • Historically, male competition was a high-stakes, zero-sum game with extreme reproductive skew, where a small percentage of dominant men fathered many children while many had none; this dynamic generated deep evolutionary motives for men to seek status, power, and hierarchy-building.
  • Female evolutionary strategies focused on non-hierarchical, supportive social networks for resource protection and childcare, utilizing relational aggression and consensus-building rather than lethal intergroup physical warfare.
  • Large-scale human cooperation and civilization building evolved as direct consequences of war, as the threat of intergroup conflict forced larger groups to organize complex institutions, technology, and social structures.
  • Humans evolved a complex integrated brain network allowing them to mentally simulate future scenarios and plan for status gains, which provides a massive strategic advantage but frequently undermines present moment happiness and peace of mind.
  • Because ancestral threats to life and reproductive success were constant, humans evolved a relentless drive for social status and control; recognizing that modern life carries far fewer survival risks allows individuals to step back from unconscious status games.

Guest

Professor Robert Pape is a political scientist who has spent 30 years studying global political violence and has advised every US White House since 9/11. He is the author of *Bombing to Win: Air Power and Coercion in War* and *Our Own Worst Enemies*. His research focuses on the causes of violent populism, suicide attacks, and strategic air power.

TLDR

The eight weeks following the US midterm elections represent a highly dangerous period marked by the potential convergence of domestic political violence, an escalating war with Iran, and a winter offensive in Ukraine. Political violence in the US is moving from the margins into the mainstream, creating combustible conditions that could be sparked by contested election results or controversial policy actions. Meanwhile, quick-strike military options like decapitation air campaigns often backfire into prolonged proxy wars, retaliatory strikes on civilian infrastructure, and inspired domestic attacks. To prevent a wider conflict, political leaders must establish strict firebreaks against unprovoked military escalation, limit federal tactical interventions in domestic cities, and enforce international bans on targeting civilian power and water grids.

Key points
  • The post-election period is susceptible to violent populism because deep political divisions combine with high-stakes transfers of power. Extensive survey research shows tens of millions of Americans support using force either to advance or block specific political outcomes, creating a highly volatile atmosphere where single rhetoric triggers can spark widespread civil unrest.
  • High-intensity air campaigns, such as a proposed three-day strategic bombing plan targeting Iranian leadership and infrastructure, rarely yield quick capitulation. Historical precedents show that such actions tend to eliminate viable negotiation partners, provoke retaliatory strikes against regional commercial nodes and airports, and lead to asymmetric threats like inspired attacks and critical infrastructure cyberwarfare.
  • The concept of the "escalation trap" explains why leaders double down on failing strategies during crises. Rather than operating like strategic chess players, leaders facing political or military setbacks behave like desperate gamblers, risking broader domestic and international instability to avoid the personal and political consequences of defeat.
  • The concurrent escalation of proxy conflicts—including Iranian drone and missile operations, cyber strikes on power grids, and Russia's strategy targeting Ukrainian utility infrastructure—threatens global supply chains, energy prices, and housing stability. If major powers begin directly striking each other's home territories or essential civilian grids, these proxy conflicts risk escalating into a broader global war.
  • Preventing catastrophic domestic and international outcomes requires explicit legislative and executive restraints, termed "firebreaks." These measures include congressional moratoriums on new offensive military operations during political transitions, requiring local gubernatorial consent before deploying armed federal agents, and international diplomatic pressure to protect civilian infrastructure.
  • Individual survival stockpiling provides limited protection against long-term grid or infrastructure collapse. Meaningful risk reduction relies on active civic engagement, public pressure on elected officials to adopt de-escalation policies, and open public discussion to counter strategic miscalculations by leadership.
