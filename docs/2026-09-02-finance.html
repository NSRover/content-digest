WTF Just Happened at Jackson Hole?
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The Federal Reserve's hawkish narrative from Jackson Hole is being contradicted by weak labor data and market indicators. While Fed officials like Kevin Warsh focus on inflation risks and suggest raising interest rates, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and market data point to a fragile labor market and a supply-driven energy shock that leads to demand destruction rather than sustained inflation. Ultimately, the bond market is not pricing in long-term inflation or rejecting Treasuries, but is instead reacting to the Fed's interference in the front end of the yield curve.

Key points
  • Federal Reserve officials are focusing heavily on inflation and taking the official unemployment rate at face value, leading them to believe the labor market is resilient enough to withstand higher interest rates. However, this ignores the Fed's other mandates and overlooks underlying labor market fragility.
  • The latest JOLTS data reveals a severe lack of hiring, with the hiring rate falling below the critical 2% threshold. Historically, when hiring drops this low, workers drop out of the labor force and are no longer counted in the official unemployment rate, meaning the true level of unemployment is being undercounted.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent argued that the current economic situation is a supply shock rather than demand-driven inflation, noting there is no evidence of energy prices spilling over into second or third-order inflation effects. This view is supported by European inflation data, where the core rate unexpectedly decelerated to 2.4% despite a jump in the headline rate.
  • Market indicators, such as TIPS break-even rates, show that the bond market is not pricing in 1970s-style inflation. Despite rising oil and gasoline prices, long-term inflation expectations remain low, signaling that the market anticipates demand destruction and disinflation rather than a wage-price spiral.
  • Rising long-term bond yields are not a sign of "Treasury rejection" or runaway inflation, as yield spreads and foreign buying data remain stable. Instead, the gently rising long-term rates are a result of yield curve mechanics and "uninversion" being disrupted by the Fed's piecemeal interest rate policies.

The Debt Endgame: Why the Dollar Must Fall and Gold Must Rise.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The G20's narrative of growing out of government debt is unrealistic because rising debt levels reduce economic productivity and non-discretionary spending continues to surge. To manage the massive projected government spending and entitlement obligations, policymakers will likely resort to inflating the debt away and implementing financial repression. A statutory revaluation of U.S. gold, similar to the 1934 revaluation, could provide temporary financial relief without increasing the national debt, but the ultimate outcome remains a weaker dollar and higher precious metals prices.

Key points
  • The G20's goal of outgrowing national debt is highly unrealistic because high debt-to-GDP ratios divert productive income toward debt servicing rather than investment and growth.
  • Non-discretionary programs like Social Security and Medicare cannot be easily canceled or reduced due to political pressure from a massive voting electorate, which now includes 73 million Social Security recipients.
  • Projected U.S. government spending is expected to rise to $69.4 trillion between 2026 and 2033, representing a 35% increase compared to the previous seven-year period.
  • To prevent a depression, the Federal Reserve must continue inflating the money supply, which will result in cost-of-living adjustments for entitlement benefits failing to keep pace with real inflation.
  • A statutory revaluation of the U.S. gold price from its current $42.22 per ounce to a level like $4,000 would devalue the dollar against a neutral asset and inject $1 trillion into the Treasury's general account without issuing new debt.
  • Despite official claims that there is no bond market crisis, the bond market is in a structural bear market, which will drive up borrowing costs for everyone and eventually force the Federal Reserve to intervene with more money printing.
