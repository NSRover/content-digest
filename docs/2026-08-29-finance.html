Understand 98% of the Eurodollar System in 18 Minutes

TLDR

The Federal Reserve and mainstream economists rely on backward-looking economic statistics because they lost the ability to accurately measure the global monetary system as it evolved into the offshore, bank-centered "Eurodollar" system. Investors can better prepare their portfolios by ignoring mainstream headlines and instead reading real-time, forward-looking probabilities expressed through a framework of interconnected market signals. By analyzing multiple market curves simultaneously, investors can distinguish temporary price shocks from permanent monetary inflation regimes to make smarter asset allocation decisions.

Key points
  • The modern monetary system has evolved beyond traditional aggregates like M1, M2, and M3 into an offshore, ledger-based Eurodollar system characterized by wholesale funding, repurchase agreements, and collateral chains. Because the Federal Reserve cannot directly measure or control this global network, it shifted its focus in the 1980s to targeting the Federal Funds rate, which suppressed the natural information conveyed by short-term money rates.
  • Mainstream economic analysis relies heavily on backward-looking lagging indicators such as the CPI, GDP, and payroll data, which only measure past outcomes and are frequently revised. In contrast, a monetary signal framework looks at real-time fingerprints left by sophisticated financial institutions actively pricing risk and liquidity needs across various market curves.
  • To avoid the pitfalls of relying on a single fallible indicator, investors should look for confirmation across multiple independent market signals, including the Treasury yield curve, TIPS curve, interest rate swaps, swap spreads, Treasury bill yields, and collateral markets. When these diverse curves align, they provide a clearer picture of whether the system is moving toward growth, reflation, stagnation, or deflation.
  • Distinguishing between temporary price shocks and sustained monetary inflation is critical for portfolio management. While events like tariffs or oil supply disruptions can temporarily spike the CPI, a lasting monetary inflation regime requires specific underlying financial conditions, which can be verified by comparing nominal Treasury yields with the TIPS curve and other funding signals.
  • Misinterpreting a temporary price shock as permanent inflation can lead to damaging portfolio mistakes, such as completely avoiding duration, overestimating how long interest rates will stay high, and over-allocating to inflation-protected assets. If the price shock actually damages consumer demand, it can ultimately lead to weaker economic growth and deflationary pressures rather than sustained inflation.

If World War III Breaks Out: What Happens to Your Money.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

Geopolitical brinkmanship between the West and the East has reached unprecedented levels, raising the possibility of a wider conflict. Looking back at the outbreak of World War I in 1914 reveals how a sudden major war can freeze the global financial system, trigger massive liquidity crises, and shut down stock exchanges and banks. Holding physical gold and silver outside of the banking system serves as vital insurance to protect wealth and maintain access to savings during such systemic disruptions.

Key points
  • Current geopolitical tensions, particularly involving the UK, Germany, and Russia over the conflict in Ukraine, have escalated to a level of brinkmanship not seen in decades, raising the risk of a broader war.
  • During the 1914 financial crisis at the start of World War I, the entire British financial system froze within 13 days, leading the government to close the London Stock Exchange for five months and shut banks for a four-day bank holiday.
  • In response to the 1914 crisis, the British government practically suspended the gold standard, began printing emergency paper treasury notes (Bradbury notes), and guaranteed the banking system to finance the war.
  • A modern-day outbreak of World War III could similarly cause the financial system to shut down, rendering paper-based assets, brokerage accounts, and ETFs temporarily inaccessible due to liquidity crises or bank closures.
  • While the global financial system has transitioned from a gold-backed standard to a fragile government debt-backed system, gold remains at the heart of global finance, as evidenced by central banks like China actively hoarding physical gold.
  • Holding physical gold and silver outside of the financial system is a prudent safeguard, as bullion dealers are likely to run out of stock immediately if a major conflict erupts.
