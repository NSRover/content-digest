Why Younger Generations Are Convinced They Are Falling Behind | Philipp Carlsson-Szlezak
gemini-flash-lite (gemini-2.5-flash-lite)fallback
TLDR

The dominant narrative that younger generations are financially falling behind older generations is not supported by the data. While younger cohorts may have different consumption patterns and delayed milestones like homeownership, their incomes and wealth are actually outpacing those of prior generations at comparable ages. This disconnect between reality and perception is fueled by inflated expectations, flawed benchmarks, and the pervasive influence of social media.

Key points
  • Data on income and wealth, adjusted for inflation and compared across age groups, show that Gen Z and Millennials are not doing worse than previous generations at similar life stages; in fact, they are often ahead.
  • Social media, particularly platforms like TikTok, contributes to distorted perceptions of financial success, with benchmarks like needing $600,000 annually to be considered financially successful being unrealistic for most.
  • Homeownership is often delayed rather than denied for younger generations, with many choosing to live in expensive urban areas initially, which impacts their ability to purchase property at the same age as previous generations.
  • Younger generations are building wealth differently, with a greater allocation to equity markets compared to real estate, which has historically provided strong returns over the long term.
  • Despite economic progress and strong consumer spending, there is record pessimism among young people, driven by a combination of social media influence, exalted expectations, and a cultural shift following the 2008 financial crisis.
  • The "K-shaped economy" narrative, suggesting a widening gap where the rich get richer and the poor get poorer, is only partially true; while the wealthy are indeed getting richer, the data also show progress in the lower and middle income distributions, though perhaps at a slower pace.

TLDR

The artificial intelligence sector shows clear signs of an expectations and credit bubble, evidenced by massive disconnects between market valuations and financial realities. A leaked Anthropic filing reveals quarterly revenue growing to $17 billion alongside $80 billion in six-month paper losses and up to $750 billion in long-term spending commitments. The inevitable correction in AI adoption will likely be driven by three major friction points: business adoption challenges, technological bottlenecks in predictive models, and credit market constraints.

Key points
  • Disruptive technological advancements historically trigger speculative cycles where capital floods into emerging markets faster than sustainable business models can develop.
  • Leaked internal numbers from Anthropic illustrate extreme financial imbalances, including tens of billions in operating and paper losses contrasted with modest revenue growth and hundreds of billions in non-cancelable long-term liabilities.
  • AI models face fundamental technical scaling limits—such as the Chinchilla scaling laws and diminishing returns from training on synthetic, AI-generated web data rather than high-quality human information.
  • Commercial enterprises struggle to implement AI productively and profitably, which pushes projected break-even dates further into the future and increases capital requirements.
  • Debt markets and credit availability represent the primary short-term constraint on the AI sector, as lenders re-evaluate risks and demanding higher yields, while broader junk bond market stress signals tightening macroeconomic liquidity.
