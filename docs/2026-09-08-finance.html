11-14% (Tax Free) returns for NRIs | (Who will pay the price?) #FCNRb
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The Reserve Bank of India's FCNR(B) deposit scheme has attracted significant US dollar inflows by offering non-resident Indians (NRIs) high leveraged returns, but it represents a costly borrowing strategy rather than true economic earning. The Indian government is using this temporary dollar buffer to stabilize a rapidly depreciating rupee and buy time to address structural growth issues like sluggish private capex and consumption slowdowns. For retail investors, this situation highlights the importance of diversifying into US dollar assets and hedging against potential rupee depreciation.

Key points
  • The FCNR(B) scheme has been marketed to NRIs with the promise of tax-free US dollar returns of 10% to 15% through the use of circular leverage, though the actual base interest rate is closer to 6% or 6.5%. This high-cost borrowing is a debt that the Indian government and banks must repay in US dollars within three to five years.
  • The primary short-term objective of the scheme is to defend the rupee from breaching the psychologically important level of 100 INR per USD, especially as the rupee's annual depreciation rate has accelerated to over 7%.
  • Some market observers view the scheme as a "dollar depreciation trade" where the RBI bets that the US dollar will weaken against the rupee by the time of repayment, though this macro bet is highly risky since India cannot control US Federal Reserve policies.
  • The underlying economic driver for the scheme is India's need for foreign exchange reserves to fund public expenditure and stimulate private capex, which has remained stagnant despite heavy government infrastructure spending since the pandemic.
  • If the scheme's underlying gamble fails to revive real GDP growth or generate long-term investor confidence, the Indian government may resort to further rupee depreciation or tighten Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) limits to restrict domestic capital from leaving the country.
  • Resident Indian investors should view these aggressive dollar-attraction schemes as a signal to diversify their portfolios internationally, particularly into US equities or physical gold, to hedge against domestic currency risks.

  • Europe faces a severe energy crisis of either shortages or bankruptcies heading into winter, exacerbated by Norway producing at maximum capacity and the strategic mistake of outsourcing refining capacity to Asia.
