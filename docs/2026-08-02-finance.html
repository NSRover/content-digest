Private Credit Defaults EXPLODE Into Public Markets
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

Fitch has reported a record high 6% private credit default rate, signaling that the credit downturn is no longer contained within private portfolios. This stress is spilling over into public markets, evidenced by triple C-rated junk bond spreads crossing 1,000 basis points, rising credit default swap prices for major AI-related companies, and falling business development company (BDC) stock prices. The credit cycle is entering a phase where both public and private lenders are actively avoiding the riskiest borrowers, challenging the narrative of a stable broader economy.

Key points
  • Fitch's latest report shows the trailing 12-month private credit default rate reached a record 6% at the end of the second quarter, with industrials and manufacturing hitting a high default rate of 10.4%.
  • Maturity extensions under stress have overtaken payment-in-kind (PIK) arrangements as the leading source of private credit defaults, indicating that borrowers are increasingly unable to repay principal on schedule rather than just struggling with interest payments.
  • Public markets are showing severe signs of risk aversion, with triple C-rated junk bond spreads pushing past 1,000 basis points, a level of crisis-like compensation that reflects an active refusal by investors to buy into the resilient economy narrative.
  • The AI buildout is transitioning into a debt market story where credit default swap (CDS) spreads for major companies like Oracle, SpaceX, Nvidia, Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet have moved higher, indicating that investors are paying more to protect against remote default risks.
  • Large private credit managers are facing liquidity constraints; Ares Capital reported a 15% quarterly increase in non-accrual investments, while Blue Owl experienced a sharp drop in credit fundraising to $1.8 billion alongside restricted withdrawals due to high redemption requests.
  • Publicly traded BDC stocks are falling toward new lows as public investors demand a margin of safety, effectively acting as a real-time, public market pricing mechanism for the underlying, opaque private credit portfolios.
  • Regulated banks are deeply connected to the private credit ecosystem through an estimated $220 billion to over $440 billion in credit lines, meaning deteriorating private loan collateral could trigger borrowing-base deficiencies and dry up broader market liquidity.

The Bond Market Is Breaking: Japan Could Trigger the Next Global Debt Crisis.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The global financial system is facing a looming sovereign debt crisis as G7 government bond yields break out of a 40-year downward trend. Japan, historically one of the largest creditors to Western nations, is shifting its policy to encourage domestic institutional investors to keep capital at home rather than financing foreign deficits. This reduction in demand for foreign debt, combined with rising interest costs and expanding fiscal deficits, is putting persistent upward pressure on global bond yields and driving a loss of control in the sovereign debt markets.

Key points
  • The 30-year US Treasury yield has broken out of a long-term triangle consolidation to reach its highest level since 2007, signaling that the 40-year bull market in bonds has reversed into a structural bear market.
  • Under Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, Japan is seeking to reverse "Abenomics" by encouraging domestic pension funds, insurers, and banks to allocate capital domestically to support the yen and increase demand for Japanese government bonds (JGBs).
  • Japan has accumulated $1.6 trillion in net foreign bond purchases since 2000, meaning even a gradual reduction in its buying of foreign debt will severely disrupt the supply-demand balance for US Treasuries, UK gilts, and European Bunds.
  • The global financial system is losing its three historical pillars of government debt demand: Japanese institutional buyers, Chinese official institutions (which are actively declining), and central bank quantitative easing.
  • The US Treasury's suggestion of intervening to keep the Japanese yen strong represents a backdoor intervention to stabilize the Treasury market, as rising hedging costs and spiking JGB yields make holding foreign debt increasingly unattractive for Japanese investors.
  • This shift toward financial fragmentation—where broke G7 nations must rely on domestic savings to fund expanding fiscal deficits—creates a highly bullish long-term backdrop for physical gold, silver, and other hard assets outside the banking system.
