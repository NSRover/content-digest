TLDR

There is currently a massive $8 trillion sitting on the sidelines in the United States, representing 26% of the economy. While historically high cash levels have preceded major recessions, recent interest rate cuts by the Federal Reserve have made cash slightly less attractive relative to stocks. This shift could incentivize institutional investors to redeploy this capital back into the market, potentially triggering a historic meltup in the S&P 500 over the next six months.

Key points
  • The $8 trillion currently sitting in money market funds is equivalent to the combined GDP of France, Spain, and Russia, representing 26% of the US economy. Historically, similar spikes in cash holdings as a share of the economy occurred right before the 1990, 2001, and 2008 recessions as institutional investors took chips off the table.
  • The decision of institutional investors to hoard cash or invest in businesses is driven by the gap between the 3-month Treasury yield (the return on cash) and the market's earnings yield (the return on stocks). When this gap narrows or turns negative, cash becomes highly attractive, reducing the incentive to allocate capital to the economy.
  • Although cash has recently been at its most attractive level since the Great Financial Crisis, the economy has remained somewhat stable despite signs of cooling, such as rising unemployment and falling consumer confidence. This stability has allowed the stock market to continue reaching new highs.
  • Recent interest rate cuts by the Federal Reserve have begun to make cash less attractive. Historical data suggests a two-year lag between changes in cash attractiveness and actual cash redeployment, indicating that the massive pile of sidelined cash may soon start flowing back into the economy and equities.
  • If this sidelined capital is redeployed, it could trigger a historic market meltup similar to the rallies seen in 2002 and 2009. However, this potential surge would occur in a stock market that is already highly concentrated and expensive, increasing the likelihood of high volatility and wild market swings.
