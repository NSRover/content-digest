BREAKING: The Jobs Report Just PLUNGED, Worse Than Everyone Expected
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The September jobs report came in well below expectations, reversing the previous month's gains and confirming a long-term trend of a weakening, fragile labor market. This lack of robust job growth has severely restricted nominal income growth, making sustained inflation impossible and leading instead to demand destruction. While the Federal Reserve mistakenly views the labor market as resilient based on a low official unemployment rate, the bond and TIPS markets are correctly pricing in a lack of inflation risk due to these underlying economic realities.

Key points
  • The September establishment survey payroll numbers were barely positive and accompanied by further downward revisions to previous months, proving that the prior month's strong report was an outlier rather than the start of a recovery.
  • The U.S. labor market is in a "forgot how to grow" contraction, sitting 9.7 million jobs below the 2010s trend line as of September 2026, which explains widespread consumer pessimism and political dissatisfaction.
  • The Federal Reserve's belief in a resilient labor market is based on the official unemployment rate, which is artificially low because it fails to count discouraged workers who have dropped out of the labor force due to a lack of hiring.
  • Nominal income growth has fallen off alongside the weakening labor market, meaning consumers lack the financial capacity to absorb higher costs, which prevents a wage-price spiral and instead triggers demand destruction when energy prices rise.
  • The TIPS and Treasury markets show near-zero inflation risk because they recognize that without strong income growth, sustained inflation cannot occur, contrasting sharply with the high-income growth era of the Great Inflation in the 1960s and 1970s.

'Big Short' Investor Explains How the AI Bubble Will Burst
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

"Big Short" investor Steve Eisman warns of a massive concentration risk in the AI sector, where the entire supply chain relies on the financial health of Anthropic and OpenAI. These two unprofitable, cash-bleeding entities generate 70% of the AI revenue for major hyperscalers, which in turn represents a massive portion of Nvidia's and Oracle's backlogs and receivables. Because the underlying LLM providers lack competitive moats and face rising competition from open-weight models, any failure or slowdown at OpenAI or Anthropic could trigger a severe, overnight recession in the United States.

Key points
  • The AI industry suffers from extreme concentration risk, starting with Nvidia, where five customers account for 70% of its accounts receivable. Further down the chain, Anthropic and OpenAI generate 70% of the AI revenue for major hyperscalers (Microsoft, Amazon, and Google), which translates to 25% to 30% of their total cloud revenue.
  • Oracle's financial health is heavily tied to OpenAI, which accounts for roughly 50% of Oracle's massive Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO) backlog. This means half of Oracle's future revenue relies on a highly unprofitable company that is bleeding cash, creating a fragile, circular financing loop where hyperscalers and Nvidia reinvest capital back into these same LLM startups.
  • Large Language Model (LLM) providers lack structural business moats because software developers can easily switch to whichever model is currently performing best. Furthermore, the era of "token maxing" (unrestricted AI usage) has ended as customers become highly cost-conscious, shifting toward cheaper, open-weight alternatives like Chinese models.
  • Eisman believes the widespread public hysteria regarding AI "ending the world" is a manufactured subterfuge by AI companies. Lacking natural moats, these firms are trying to scare the government into passing heavy regulations that would legally block foreign open-weight competitors and artificially establish a domestic duopoly.
  • For retail investors looking to participate in AI, Eisman advises avoiding LLM providers entirely and being cautious with hyperscalers, whose massive capital expenditures have turned their once-abundant cash flows negative. Instead, he favors "picks and shovels" plays that capture this capital expenditure, such as Nvidia, Micron, GE Vernova, Arista Networks, Cisco, and Eaton.
