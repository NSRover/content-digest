If You Don’t Understand Bonds, You Don’t Understand Why Stocks Are Crashing
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The common narrative that rising Federal Reserve interest rates and higher discount rates are causing stock market sell-offs is incorrect; rather, markets are reacting to underlying economic weaknesses long telegraphed by the yield curve. A yield curve that is steep at the front end but flat at the back shows that while the Fed remains focused on near-term energy and inflation risks, long-term economic growth and reflation are absent. Recent global risk-off liquidations across emerging markets, technology stocks, and industrial commodities like copper and silver demonstrate that current economic fundamentals cannot support current risk valuations.

Key points
  • The front end of the yield curve reflects Federal Reserve policy risks, while the flat back end reflects low long-term growth and inflation expectations, failing to validate claims of an AI-driven economic reflation.
  • Falling TIPS break-even rates—with the 10-year break-even setting a new two-year low—contradict the argument that rising nominal yields are being driven by broad, unanchored inflation.
  • High-profile events like the Anthropic IPO failed to sustain broader risk-taking because market participants are increasingly questioning whether heavy capital spending on AI will translate into near-term macroeconomic strength.
  • Sharp sell-offs and liquidations in copper and silver, occurring despite tight supply constraints, confirm that real-world industrial demand and speculative optimism are deteriorating rapidly.
  • Severe weakness is expanding globally through correlated risk-off price action, marked by steep declines in emerging market equities, sharp drops in tech-heavy Asian indexes like Taiwan and South Korea, and persistent pressure on the NASDAQ.
  • A growing divergence between Treasury bills pricing in Fed rate hikes and term SOFR hedging against them indicates market doubt over whether the financial system can tolerate further monetary tightening.
