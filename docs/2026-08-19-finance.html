30-Year Bond Yields Are Surging... Here’s What It Means
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The 30-year U.S. Treasury yield recently reached its highest level since 2007 at approximately 5.31%, sparking mainstream narratives of runaway inflation, fiscal collapse, and bond vigilantes. However, this yield is only about 20 basis points higher than its October 2023 peak, indicating a sideways trading range rather than a market revolution. The movement actually reflects ordinary yield curve normalization and bull steepening rather than a systemic rejection of government debt.

Key points
  • The mainstream narrative attributes the rise in the 30-year yield to massive government deficits, persistent inflation, and a strike by foreign buyers. This identical panic occurred in October 2023 when the yield crossed 5%, only for yields to plunge by over a full percentage point shortly after.
  • A 5% yield is only historically high if compared strictly to the post-2008 era of unusually low rates. Throughout the 1990s, the 30-year yield sat between 6% and 8% during a period of strong economic growth, meaning current levels are ordinary by long-term historical standards.
  • The rest of the yield curve does not support the inflation or tightening narrative. The two-year Treasury yield has been moving lower due to softening economic data, such as weak retail sales and negative labor market indicators, which points to bull steepening rather than an economy-wide inflation repricing.
  • True bond vigilantism is not occurring because the market is not demanding a rapidly expanding premium for holding long-term debt. The spread between the 10-year and 30-year yields remains flat, and the spread between the 30-year and short-term rates remains historically low.
  • Short-term auction volatility and reported declines in official foreign holdings are being misinterpreting as a structural buyer strike. Historically, even during major disruptions like the 2011 U.S. debt downgrade, macroeconomic fundamentals eventually overwhelmed short-term supply dynamics to drive yields lower.

Japan and the US Just Pulled the Trigger (Brace for Impact)

TLDR

Japan and the United States have launched a massive, coordinated currency intervention to defend the weakening Japanese yen, with Japan spending $60 billion and the US spending $10 billion. Historically, sudden strengthening of the yen has served as a leading indicator for major US stock market crashes, such as in 1987, 2001, and 2008, due to the unwinding of the massive yen carry trade. The US was forced to intervene because Japan, the largest foreign holder of US debt, has begun selling off US Treasuries to support its currency, driving US bond yields higher and squeezing the US government's budget.

Key points
  • Japan and the US executed a historic joint intervention to support the yen, which had fallen to its weakest level against the dollar in over four decades. Japan spent roughly $60 billion and the US spent $10 billion in a single day, triggering one of the largest single-day moves in the currency's history.
  • A rapidly strengthening yen has historically been a "canary in the coal mine" for US financial markets, preceding the 1987 Black Monday crash, the 2001 dot-com bust, and the 2008 financial crisis. In these past episodes, the stock market ultimately fell by 30% to over 50% as the yen gained strength.
  • The connection between the yen and global markets lies in the "yen carry trade," where investors borrow yen at near-zero interest rates to buy higher-yielding assets like US stocks and Treasuries. This trade is estimated to tie up as much as $14 trillion in global markets, meaning a sudden rise in the yen could trigger a massive short squeeze and forced asset liquidations.
  • The US intervened not out of friendship, but to protect its own bond market. As the largest foreign holder of US debt with over $1.1 trillion in Treasuries, Japan has started dumping these assets—selling over $100 billion since the start of 2026—to buy yen, which has pushed the 30-year US Treasury yield above 5% for the first time since July 2007.
  • While a structural reversal in the yen (such as the Bank of Japan raising interest rates to 3%) poses a severe systemic risk to US asset prices, the current intervention is viewed as a temporary band-aid. An immediate systemic collapse is not the base case for the coming months, but the risk of heightened market volatility is steadily rising.

How to Invest as an NRI? (in India & abroad) | Akshat Shrivastava
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

For Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) planning to return to India, a successful investment strategy requires separating where they live from where their wealth is stored. Keeping a significant portion of wealth outside India ensures asset mobility for future family expenses, protects against currency depreciation, and simplifies succession planning. Returning NRIs should focus on liquid assets, optimize their portfolios to minimize high tax slabs, and carefully evaluate the long-term implications of buying illiquid real estate.

Key points
  • Wealth storage and mobility are distinct from residency. Even if an NRI decides to retire in India, keeping a large portion of their wealth in global hubs like the UAE or Singapore is highly beneficial. Once an NRI transitions to a resident Indian status, moving large sums of money out of India legally becomes highly restricted, which can complicate funding global expenses like children's education.
  • Currency depreciation and growth favor keeping assets abroad. Historically, the Indian Rupee (INR) depreciates against the US Dollar (USD) over long horizons. Storing wealth in multi-currency accounts abroad protects against this depreciation while allowing investors to capture global market growth.
  • Optimize cash flow strategies to minimize taxation. Returning NRIs must plan their retirement withdrawals carefully to avoid high tax brackets. While interest from fixed deposits is taxed at slab rates (up to 30%+), structuring withdrawals through equities and systematic withdrawal plans (SWP) can lower the tax burden to long-term capital gains rates.
  • Avoid buying illiquid real estate abroad if returning to India. Purchasing property in high-tax, high-maintenance regions like the US, Australia, or Europe makes little sense for those planning to relocate. Managing foreign real estate from India presents severe operational challenges, and delaying property purchases in favor of liquid assets is generally recommended.
  • Succession planning must account for children's future residency. Since the children of most returning NRIs choose to live abroad, leaving them illiquid Indian real estate can create massive liquidation and repatriation hurdles. It is more practical to hold wealth in highly liquid, globally accessible survival assets like US stocks.
  • A 0% tax setup is possible through strategic residency. By establishing tax residency in jurisdictions with 0% capital gains tax, such as the UAE, and limiting physical stay in India to under 120 days a year, global investors can legally optimize their tax liabilities on stock market and options income.

1,000 Years of Currency Debasement: Vikings, Silver & Fiat Currency.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

Currency debasement is a historical constant, occurring today through the fiat monetary system and government debt markets just as it did 1,000 years ago with physical coinage. In Viking Norway, King Harald Hardrada drastically reduced the silver content of his coins to one-third of their original value, provoking severe backlash from his subjects. Today, rising global bond yields and falling bond prices represent a modern form of debasement, highlighting the enduring role of gold and silver as sound money.

Key points
  • Modern currency debasement occurs through a fiat system built on debt and credit rather than physical metal. Because all fiat currency is backed by government debt, the rapid loss of value in government bonds directly translates to a debasement of the currency itself.
  • Global bond markets are showing signs of deterioration, with the US 10-year Treasury yield approaching 5% and the 30-year yield reaching its highest level since 2007. Similar upward yield pressures are visible in the UK, Europe, and Japan, where the 30-year yield has risen to 4.13% alongside a weakening yen.
  • Rising yields are weighing heavily on the stock market, but gold, silver, and oil prices have recently risen. This upward movement in precious metals during a bond bear market suggests investors are executing a "debasement trade" to seek refuge in sound money.
  • Historical precedents, such as the debasement of Brazilian silver coins between 1889 and 1922, demonstrate how governments systematically shrink coin sizes and lower precious metal content to inflate the currency supply.
  • A historical account from the Norges Bank reveals that King Harald Hardrada of Norway (1045–1066) initiated the country's first major coin debasement by cutting the silver content of his coins to just one-third. This debasement, which produced coins known as "Harold's Slatton," angered Viking soldiers who demanded real silver under threat of violence.
