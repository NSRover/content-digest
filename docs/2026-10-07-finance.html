Jamie Dimon Just Made A Shocking Interest Rate Prediction (Not What You Think)
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon has warned that sticky inflation could push interest rates higher, but historical patterns suggest his public warnings often precede a significant downward turn in Treasury yields. While Dimon publicly promotes a "higher for longer" rate narrative, JPMorgan has historically increased its holdings of Treasury and agency securities during these exact periods. Current market indicators, including the easing of September seasonal pressures and actions by major institutional investors, suggest the bond market may be approaching a peak in yields rather than a sustained upward trend.

Key points
  • Jamie Dimon's public warnings about rising inflation and interest rates have historically coincided with major turning points where Treasury yields actually fell significantly, such as in 2018 and 2024.
  • Despite Dimon's public warnings of higher rates, JPMorgan dramatically increased its own holdings of Treasury and agency securities by 153% between March 2018 and June 2019, and by 39% in 2024.
  • The sharp rise in Treasury yields during September was largely driven by temporary seasonal trends, funding bottlenecks, and dealer balance sheet constraints rather than a fundamental shift in long-term inflation.
  • Major institutional investors are shifting their stance; PIMCO is reducing its underweight position in long-term government debt, and Man Group research indicates that moderate inflation of 2% to 4% historically yields positive returns for 10-year Treasuries.
  • Market indicators like the TIPS market, inflation swaps, and the flat front-end of the yield curve show no signs of pricing in the sustained, aggressive inflationary cycle required to support Dimon's rate prediction.

The Dollar Index Is Lying to You — Here’s What Really Matters.
gemini-flash (gemini-2.5-flash)fallback
TLDR

The Dollar Index (DXY) is an increasingly irrelevant measure of the dollar's true strength and purchasing power because it heavily weights declining European and Japanese currencies while ignoring major global players like the Chinese yuan. Since 2008, the dollar has actually weakened against the yuan, and gold has significantly outperformed the DXY, indicating a different reality than the index suggests. The ongoing debasement of fiat currencies and rising bond yields in Western economies further highlight the dollar's underlying weakness and the importance of gold as monetary insurance.

Key points
  • The Dollar Index (DXY) was created in 1973 after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the end of dollar convertibility to gold, to measure the value of floating fiat currencies against each other.
  • The DXY is heavily weighted towards the Euro, Japanese Yen, and British Pound, which together comprise 84.1% of the index, while completely excluding the Chinese yuan and other BRICS currencies.
  • Since 2008, the global economic landscape has shifted significantly, with the US share of global GDP increasing slightly, China's share more than doubling, and the Eurozone, Japan, and the UK seeing their shares decline.
  • The dollar has actually weakened against the Chinese yuan since 2008, falling from over 7 yuan per dollar to 6.70 yuan, a trend that is expected to continue.
  • Gold has consistently risen since 2008, while the DXY has trended upwards against a basket of underperforming currencies, demonstrating that gold is a better indicator of the dollar's true purchasing power than the DXY.
  • Rising bond yields in the US and Europe are impacting corporate America and the broader economy, indicating underlying financial stress, while China's bond market is in a different cycle.
  • Inflation is a monetary phenomenon caused by the expansion of the money supply, not rising fuel prices, and fiat currencies continue to depreciate against gold, making gold essential monetary insurance.
