Guest

Dr. Darby Saxbe is a clinical psychologist, professor at USC, and author. She runs a research lab that studies how the transition to parenthood affects the brains, hormones, and relationships of both mothers and fathers.

TLDR

Becoming a father triggers significant neurological and hormonal changes, including a loss of gray matter volume and a drop in testosterone, which are directly tied to hands-on caregiving. While mothers are biologically primed for parenthood, a father's brain adaptation is a flexible response driven by active participation, bonding, and cultural expectations. Recognizing paternal postpartum depression and supporting involved fatherhood is not a zero-sum game; rather, it is a vital step that directly improves maternal mental health, child development, and family well-being.

Key points
  • Research shows that first-time fathers lose about 1% of their gray matter volume across the transition to parenthood, particularly in the cortex. This structural change is a pruning process that optimizes the brain for baby-related memory, empathy, and caregiving, though it may temporarily compromise short-term working memory for non-baby tasks.
  • Unlike mothers, who are biologically primed for caregiving by the end of pregnancy, fathers possess a "caregiving dial" that is set in the middle. A father's neurological and hormonal adaptation is a flexible response that turns up or down based on culture, choice, and active, hands-on parenting experiences like diapering, feeding, and bathing.
  • Active fatherhood alters male hormones, specifically causing a drop in testosterone levels around the birth of a baby. This hormonal shift is adaptive, redirecting a male's focus away from mating competition and aggression toward nurturing, pair-bonding, and offspring survival, which also correlates with reduced risk-taking and increased conscientiousness.
  • A study on how child gender influences fathers found that fathers of daughters experience a greater drop in testosterone, spend less time caring for their babies, and report more parenting stress and worse bonding compared to fathers of sons. This suggests that fathers of daughters may require additional coaching and preparation to engage effectively.
  • Postpartum depression affects approximately 10% of new fathers—about twice the rate of depression in the general male population. Risk factors include sleep deprivation, relationship strain, identity shifts, and evolutionary-cultural mismatch, as modern nuclear families lack the cooperative community support systems humans evolved with.
  • Supporting paternal mental health and expanding paternity leave directly benefits mothers. Studies show that increased paternal involvement and flexible paternity leave lead to lower rates of postpartum depression and fewer anti-anxiety medication prescriptions for mothers, demonstrating that family mental health is interconnected rather than zero-sum.

  • "Project Fatherhood": A program directed at Homeboy Industries in Los Angeles that offers parenting classes and support for fathers, including those transitioning back into society after incarceration.

Guest

Dr. Stasha Gominak is a neurologist who previously served as a clinical assistant professor of neurology at Stanford University Hospital and completed her residency at Harvard Medical School. She practiced neurology for over 20 years, during which she developed a specialized focus on the chemical and biological root causes of sleep disorders. She is the creator of the RightSleep protocol, a method she now teaches full-time to help patients restore their sleep through brain chemistry and microbiome health.

TLDR

Modern sleep disorders and chronic diseases are primarily driven by a chemical deficiency state caused by our shift to indoor living, which deprives the body of natural vitamin D and disrupts the gut microbiome's ability to produce essential B vitamins. When vitamin D levels drop, the brain stem loses the chemical signals required to enter deep, restorative sleep states like REM, preventing the body from performing its nightly repairs. By systematically correcting vitamin D levels and temporarily supplementing B vitamins to jumpstart the gut microbiome, individuals can restore their natural sleep cycles and reverse chronic inflammatory conditions.

Key points
  • The widespread rise in sleep disorders, ADHD, depression, and metabolic diseases since the 1980s coincides with humanity's shift to indoor living, air conditioning, and the widespread use of sunscreen, which created a systemic hormone deficiency.
  • Vitamin D is actually a hormone rather than a vitamin, and it acts as a "hibernation hormone" that binds to receptors in the brain stem to control sleep transitions and paralysis; without it, the brain physically forgets how to sleep.
  • The parasympathetic nervous system relies on the neurotransmitter acetylcholine to drive deep sleep and bodily repair, but the body cannot produce the enzyme required to make acetylcholine without adequate levels of vitamin D.
  • There is a critical, symbiotic relationship between vitamin D and the gut microbiome, where the body must provide vitamin D to the gut bacteria so they can, in turn, manufacture all eight essential B vitamins.
  • Supplementing with vitamin D alone over a long period is a form of biohacking that can be dangerous, as it burns through the body's B vitamin stores and eventually triggers secondary B vitamin deficiencies, leading to symptoms like burning extremities, insomnia, and inflammation.
  • To repair a damaged gut microbiome and restore sleep, the "RightSleep" protocol recommends raising vitamin D to a target blood level above 60 ng/mL while temporarily taking a high-dose B-complex (like B50) for three months to jumpstart the gut bacteria before tapering off.
  • Natural sunlight cannot be fully replaced by oral supplements or modern indoor lighting, as sunlight hitting cholesterol in the skin produces sulfated vitamin D alongside 15 to 20 other anti-inflammatory chemicals, while old-school incandescent bulbs provide beneficial near-infrared light that modern LEDs lack.
