TLDR

The global economy is facing a severe energy shock with rising oil and gasoline prices, which central bankers are misinterpreting as an inflationary threat requiring further interest rate hikes. However, market signals like a flattening yield curve and real-world volume data show that high energy costs are actually driving widespread demand destruction rather than second-round inflation. This demand destruction is already eroding physical fuel consumption and consumer confidence, pointing to an inevitable economic downturn rather than a sustained inflationary spiral.

Key points
  • The August CPI report showed rising energy prices but no evidence of broadening second-round inflationary effects in the core rate, as weak overall demand prevents service providers from successfully passing on higher input costs.
  • Despite weak underlying trends, the Federal Reserve and other central banks are expected to continue raising short-term interest rates due to fears of sticky energy prices, a move priced in by the highly sensitive Treasury bill and term SOFR markets.
  • While short-term rates rise, long-term yields remain relatively flat, causing the yield curve to flatten or prepare to reinvert; this market signal indicates that rate hikes in a fragile environment will only accelerate the transition to economic contraction.
  • Real-world data from the EIA and IEA confirm significant demand destruction, with US petroleum volume usage down nearly 4% year-over-year and the IEA forecasting a global demand decline of 2.5 million barrels per day for 2026.
  • Physical gasoline volumes are declining for the second summer in a row, illustrated by retailers like Circle K reporting a 33% increase in nominal dollar sales alongside a 2% drop in actual fuel volume sold.
  • The ongoing energy shock and rising interest rates are shifting consumer anxiety from price inflation to labor market stability, with consumer surveys showing the highest expectations for rising unemployment since April 2020.

TLDR

The bond market is aggressively testing policymakers as Treasury buybacks fail to suppress rising long-end yields amid hot nominal GDP and energy-driven inflation. Investors are bracing for a highly anticipated Federal Reserve meeting, where a potential "credibility hike" could paradoxically ease long-end yields by signaling inflation resolve. Meanwhile, the broader economic outlook is clouded by slowing AI-led growth, political backlash against data centers, and the persistent appeal of debasement trades despite short-term volatility.

Key points
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's announcement of $6 billion in long-end buybacks—above the initial $4 billion guidance—failed to stop a bond selloff, signaling that the market demands much more aggressive intervention to suppress yields.
  • A regression of the 10-year Treasury yield to nominal GDP suggests a fair value of around 5.8%, meaning policymakers are fighting an uphill battle to keep a lid on yields while nominal growth remains hot.
  • The Federal Reserve faces a difficult choice between hiking rates into an energy supply shock, which historically risks triggering a recession, or letting inflation run hot by keeping financial conditions loose ahead of the midterm elections.
  • If the Fed delivers a single "credibility hike" to appease hawkish committee members, historical precedence suggests that long-end yields could actually fall as term premium pressures ease.
  • The dominant AI and semiconductor investment trades are showing signs of peaking growth and facing bipartisan political backlash, making them highly leveraged "dead money" that may require months of consolidation.
  • Despite short-term downside risks and path volatility, the long-term thesis for debasement trades remains intact due to structural inflation, high fiscal spending, and the likelihood of further government intervention.

The Bond Market Crisis Is Coming for Everyone.
gemini-3-5-flash (gemini-3.5-flash)
TLDR

The global bond market has entered a long-term bear market, reversing a 40-year trend of falling interest rates and cheap credit. This shift is driving government bond yields higher, which will trigger a sovereign debt crisis across the G7 nations and negatively impact mortgages, house prices, corporate borrowing, and pension funds. To protect savings from the resulting instability in the debt-based monetary system, investors should hold physical gold and silver outside of the financial system.

Key points
  • The 40-year bull market in bonds that began in 1981 has officially ended, marking the start of a long-term bond bear market that could last another 15 to 20 years.
  • Rising Treasury yields are creating an interest expense trap for the US government, as maturing low-rate debt must be refinanced at much higher rates, absorbing a larger share of federal revenue.
  • Higher bond yields directly translate into increased mortgage rates, which will deteriorate housing affordability, slow down real estate turnover, and put downward pressure on property valuations.
  • Businesses of all sizes face higher borrowing costs and squeezed profit margins, with small and medium-sized enterprises being hit particularly hard due to their heavy reliance on banks and private credit.
  • The decline in bond values threatens the balance sheets of banks and pension funds, which hold large volumes of bonds as safe assets, potentially compromising their ability to meet future liabilities.
  • The current fiat monetary system is debt-based and relies on government bonds as collateral; as these bonds lose value, the stability of the entire financial system is put under serious threat.
