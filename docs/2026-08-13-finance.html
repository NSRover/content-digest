Japan’s Yen Intervention Just Failed...Now What?

TLDR

A massive joint currency intervention by Japan and the United States failed to sustain the yen's recovery because it only targeted short-term speculators rather than the underlying structural issues. The yen's persistent weakness is driven by Japanese institutional capital continuously seeking better risk-adjusted returns abroad due to a fragile domestic economy, weak household demand, and demographic pressures. Raising interest rates to defend the currency risks destabilizing the Japanese government bond market and worsening domestic economic conditions.

Key points
  • The joint intervention by Japan and the United States temporarily squeezed speculative short positions, but the yen resumed its decline shortly after hedge funds cut their bearish bets in half. This demonstrates that speculators are not the primary cause of the currency's weakness, and official buying cannot alter the structural demand for dollars.
  • Japan's inflation is driven by a weak currency and import price shocks rather than robust domestic growth or wage increases. This imported inflation acts as a tax on households, forcing them to cut discretionary spending and further weakening the domestic economy.
  • The conventional view that narrowing the interest rate differential will stabilize the yen is flawed. Despite the Bank of Japan raising rates and narrowing the yield gap with US Treasuries, the yen has continued to weaken because higher domestic yields increase volatility and threaten massive mark-to-market losses for institutions holding Japanese government bonds.
  • The true "carry trade" is driven by Japanese institutional investors—such as pension funds and insurance companies—who must invest their massive savings abroad to meet long-term liabilities because the domestic economy lacks attractive, risk-adjusted investment opportunities.
  • A growing policy rift is emerging between Washington and Tokyo. US officials advocate for aggressive rate hikes to support the yen, while Japanese policymakers fear that higher rates will destabilize the domestic bond market and further harm an already fragile economy.

TLDR

Britain has a historical pattern of major financial crises occurring around the autumn equinox, including abandoning the gold standard in September 1931, exiting the ERM in September 1992, and the gilt market collapse in September 2022. In 2026, UK gilt yields are again approaching critical levels, with a potential break above 5.20% threatening to trigger a dangerous feedback loop of rising borrowing costs and debt-service expenses. If a new bond crisis erupts in the UK, it could rapidly spread to other G7 nations and impact the global financial system.

Key points
  • Historically, Britain has suffered severe financial crises during the autumn season, such as the run on sterling that forced the country off the gold standard on September 21, 1931, and "Black Wednesday" on September 16, 1992, when the UK was forced out of the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM).
  • The UK gilt market experienced a major crisis on September 23, 2022, following the Truss-Kwarteng mini-budget, which caused sterling to drop sharply and forced the Bank of England to intervene to save leveraged pension strategies.
  • In 2026, UK 10-year gilt yields are testing the 5% level and sitting well above the peak of the 2022 crisis, signaling that bond investors lack confidence in the British state's ability to manage its intractable debt.
  • A decisive breakout above the 5.20% threshold on gilts would indicate that the market is demanding a structurally higher risk premium, which would drive up mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and the government's own debt-refinancing expenses.
  • This potential 2026 crisis represents a battle between the bond market and Britain's overall fiscal position, which could ultimately force the Bank of England to inject more liquidity to prevent a total collapse.
